Title: Grow your (Clojure) codebases
Date: 2023-07-04
Tags: clojure

Like I said on the previous post, I will ramble a bit about a few things I think
are paramount when working in teams, in particular with clojure code bases. But
this can apply to other languages.

It's quite common from 1-10 people code bases to have to go through a rough
phase where a lot has to be re-considered to be able to make your engineering
folks be happier but most importantly more productive down the road. 

That might seem useless to some; just use microservices and let people do what
they want right? Well, as it happens people like to move accross teams, SRE like
not to have to dig into 200 flavors of code structure/style while fire-fighting,
and the l̶a̶z̶y̶ perfectionists, like yours truly, like when things are somewhat
consistent.

## Rule #1: Do not rush into it

That might seem obvious, but it's very common. Someone takes the reins and does
it all, a few review the thing, and poof, we have the new ISO standard to do
things for the next 5 years at least. If you're lucky, the person who did the
job has it all right on the first shot, but it's rarely the case in my
experience (I have been the guilty party on this one as well in the past).

Not every team as the same needs, seniority or works on wildly different things
making standardizing tricky.

Something important to keep in mind also, is that once rules are set in stone
and 10+ teams adopt them, going back to the drawing board can impact a lot of
people. You generally don't want your roadmap littered with "refactoring Foo"
for no good reason other than making it adopt the latest iteration of the grand
design for these things.

Like I said, don't rush, talk about it with others. 

On that last point: talking about it, that's a great ramp for the next bit.

## Rule #2: 

On talking about it. People do not like to review a document describing an Onion
with 250 layers. Try to keep it simple, I mean the whole design, not the
document. 

Not only for reviewers but for users. Generally adopting a few strong
architecture principles, a repository structure and shared tooling is good
enough. That might be easier said than done. I can give a few examples of what
I consider red flags.

Red flags: 
* more than one way to achieve the same task, think about the folks who will
  have to know about all of them when they juggle from repo to repo. It's a
  major pain. Adopt one and stick with it. In clojure land that might mean have
  a function to do task A, with a macro that just saves maybe writting a few
  extra chars using the same function and another one that allow composing
  multiple calls of the same function. Just provide the function alone, even if
  that means a bit more typing here and there for folks using it.
